Introduction

CONTENT WARNING: This article discusses cyber extortion and "sextortion." Due to the sensitive nature of this case, I have omitted the victim's name out of respect for their privacy.

Phishing remains one of the most prevalent attack vectors used by cybercriminals to trick targets into divulging credentials, clicking malicious links, or executing malware. As defensive controls improve, these social engineering campaigns continuously evolve in sophistication—making them increasingly difficult for the average user to spot.

In this write-up, I walk through a real-world investigation that began when a peer asked me to analyze a suspicious email they received. What started as a quick triage evolved into an OSINT dive that allowed me to trace a scammer's infrastructure directly back to an individual in Russia. While I was initially hesitant to publish this, I received explicit consent from my peer (referred to as "MT") to share our findings to help educate others on how these extortion schemes operate.

1. Initial Contact and Triage

On July 10, 2025, MT reached out regarding a alarming email sitting in their inbox. Knowing my background in digital forensics and security research, they asked if I would be willing to analyze it.

The message immediately raised red flags for MT because the From header displayed their own email address, creating the illusion that the email had been sent directly from their compromised account.



Fearing an account takeover, MT opened the message. The email contained a classic sextortion script: the sender claimed to have compromised MT’s device, installed spyware, recorded explicit footage via webcam, and exfiltrated their contact list. The attacker demanded a ransom paid in Bitcoin to prevent the public release of the media.

I reassured MT that these high-volume automated campaigns almost exclusively rely on fear and leaked password databases rather than an actual endpoint compromise. I immediately initiated a formal forensic analysis.

2. Context Gathering and Initial Scoping

Before diving into raw email headers, I interviewed MT to establish a baseline, eliminate potential attack vectors, and assess the true scope of the situation. I assured them that this was a non-judgmental space and that accurate details were critical to the investigation.

I focused on four key questions:

  • Did any specific claims in the email contain granular, non-public truths?
  • Had they interacted with suspicious links, downloaded unexpected attachments, or visited untrusted domains recently?
  • Were there any unusual login events or unknown active sessions in their account audit logs?
  • Were there any sent messages in their outbox that they did not author?

MT confirmed that all claims were entirely fabricated, no unexpected session logs were present, and no unauthorized outbound messages existed. This allowed me to pivot away from an active endpoint or session-hijacking incident response scenario and focus on analyzing the email's origin and header transport path.

3. Data Breach Exposure & Header Analysis

First, I checked whether MT's email address had been leaked in historical data breaches using Have I Been Pwned.



The address appeared in nineteen (19) distinct breach datasets spanning from December 2012 to recent months. MT clarified that this address was their legacy "junk/subscriptions" inbox. This explained how the threat actor obtained the target address—likely buying or scraping public breach dumps to feed automated mass-mailing scripts.

Next, I requested the raw RFC 822 email source code to analyze the email headers.



I inspected the hop-by-hop Received headers to trace the transport route from the originating Mail Transfer Agent (MTA) to MT's inbox provider.

Key Header Discoveries:

  • Inbound Gateway Node: DU2PEPF00028D06.europrd03.prod.outlook.com (Microsoft Exchange Online Infrastructure)
  • Connecting Domain: willowstonecanyon.org
  • Originating IPv4 Address: 46.232.41.252




Examining the authentication results header revealed a critical finding: received-spf: fail. The SPF check failed because the originating IP (46.232.41.252) was not designated as an authorized sender in the SPF record for MT's domain or the claimed sending domain. The sender had simply spoofed the display name and From header field to trick the mail client into showing the recipient's own address.

4. Infrastructure Enumeration & WHOIS Deep Dive

With the originating IP address (46.232.41.252) isolated, I conducted active and passive network reconnaissance.

An initial TCP port scan and ICMP echo request against the target host timed out, suggesting the host was either offline or dropping unsolicited traffic at the firewall level. I then turned to passive registration data using whois.

Execution: whois 46.232.41.252



The primary abuse contact listed was pavel_chachin@keemail.me. The use of keemail.me—a privacy-focused, encrypted mail domain associated with Tuta (formerly Tutanota)—is atypical for a legitimate Local Internet Registry (LIR) or enterprise network maintainer, often signaling an intentional effort to obscure identity.



Crucially, the record contained the status line: remarks: Pending deregistration by the RIPE NCC. The RIPE NCC (the Regional Internet Registry for Europe, the Middle East, and parts of Central Asia) was actively revoking this IP block due to policy non-compliance, non-payment, or unverified organization details.

The network allocation identifier (netname: RU-DOMTEHNIKI-NET-20110209) provided further metadata:

  • Country Code: RU (Russian Federation)
  • Org Identifier: Associated with LIR entry "Dom Tehniki Ltd"
  • Allocation Date: February 9, 2011

Checking the RIPE database routing maintainer objects revealed full administrative control delegated to a specific maintainer handle (chachinmnt):
  • mnt-by: chachinmnt
  • mnt-lower: chachinmnt
  • mnt-routes: chachinmnt / mnt-md-alexhost-1

Note: The inclusion of mnt-md-alexhost-1 links the routing object to AlexHost, a Moldova-based offshore hosting provider commonly associated with bulletproof hosting services.



Cross-referencing the physical business address listed under the LIR record ("Dom Tehniki Ltd") against public records showed discrepancies with the actual commercial entity of the same name, indicating that an enterprise registration or stolen LIR object was likely being misused to anchor this IP space.



Finally, the direct contact handle (nic-hdl: TB4584-RIPE) revealed explicit registrant details:
  • Person: Pavel Chachin
  • Physical Address: Street Prigorodnaya, 10-38, Omsk, Russian Federation
  • Phone Number: +73833999950

I now had a solid set of identifiers linked to the infrastructure owner: an individual name, location, phone number, handle, and email address.

5. OSINT Pivoting & Threat Profiling

Using the selector "Pavel Chachin" alongside the geographic anchor (Omsk, Russia), I initiated passive open-source intelligence (OSINT) searches.

The query surfaced indexed public assets matching the exact identity:

  • Facebook Profile: https://www.facebook.com/pavel.chachin.7/
  • Prezi Presentation: https://prezi.com/rolmmyabdhim/11111/



To expand the target footprint, I extracted the handle candidate (pavel.chachin.7) and queried registered social platforms using Sherlock.

Execution: python3 sherlock pavel.chachin.7 --print-found --disable-update-check



The enumeration returned multiple matching accounts across Eastern European forums and global web services. After manually validating response codes to eliminate false positives, I compiled a dossier establishing a strong correlation between the individual in Omsk and the owner of the IP space hosting the scam infrastructure.

6. Reporting & Victim Remediation

I compiled the complete forensic analysis into a formal incident report for MT, accompanying it with an archive containing raw email headers, WHOIS lookup logs, traceroutes, and verified OSINT findings.

The report outlined the following conclusions:

  1. No Account Takeover: The email was a display-name and header spoof originating from external infrastructure (46.232.41.252); MT's credentials and endpoint remained secure.
  2. Automated Campaign: The attack leveraged leaked credentials from historic third-party data breaches to target MT's secondary email.
  3. Attacker Profile: The infrastructure was tied to a flagged, deregistering IP block under the administrative control of a known individual operating out of Russia.

I provided MT with actionable security recommendations, including enforcing strict Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA), monitoring secondary email accounts for unusual access, and disregarding future extortion claims.

Conclusion & Key Takeaways

Analyzing real-world attack infrastructure offers practical insights into social engineering tactics and network-level indicators that synthetic lab environments rarely replicate. This case highlights how threat actors abuse legacy LIR allocations, bulletproof hosting routes, and email header spoofing to execute mass extortion campaigns.

This investigation also directly inspired the development of my project, PhishTrap a browser extension and defensive toolkit built to streamline email header analysis, extract connecting IPs automatically, and educate end users against phishing vectors.

Post-Investigation Update:
As of this writing, the infrastructure identified in this case—including the specific IP range, primary abuse mailer, and associated cryptocurrency receiving wallet has been de-indexed, blocked across major threat feeds, and deregistered by RIPE NCC.
